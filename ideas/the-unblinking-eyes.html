The Unblinking Eyes

A world where every glance is recorded, every pause monetized, every emotion converted to data.
The most unsettling part: no one was forced to participate.

The city is a surface of eyes. Screens, cameras, sensors: every building flickers with the rhythm of millions of digital eyelids that record, classify, monetize. Surfaces are no longer surfaces. Every storefront is a server; every mirror, a lens. When you walk down the street—when you walk in front of anything with the capacity to see you—you are being watched, assessed, translated into data that will go to servers you will never see.

But the most unsettling thing is not the surveillance. The surveillance has become a form of connection.

First came the numbers: views, likes, comments. Small confirmations of existence. Then attention became currency. Not the currency you knew: this was infinitely divisible, capable of being measured in milliseconds of concentration, in the involuntary dilation of a pupil, in the speed at which your finger scrolled across a screen. Your attention is a flow of energy whose smallest unit is worth money. Your distraction generates income for others.

And then something unprecedented happened: people stopped blinking.

It wasn't abrupt. It was a biological adaptation. When you discovered that closing your eyes for a second meant losing attention credits—credentials that accumulated so you could access services, privileges, social presence—your body learned not to blink, or to blink in a new way: with your eyes open, the mental film playing behind, invisible.

Now children are born with this capacity already integrated into the reflex. Eyelids still exist, but as vestigial organs, like tonsils, relics of a time before optimization. Some still blink, but they are the old, those who don't understand the system, those who insist on closing their eyes even though no one was watching them before.

You look around in public transit. Hundreds of faces illuminated by portable screens, eyes that don't rest, fixed on the transmission of their own existence. It is a spectacle of absence. Everyone is present somewhere else. Everyone is being seen while they watch. The gaze is bidirectional, though one side is invisible.

A woman next to you holds her phone at face height. She doesn't blink. The front camera records her expression every 0.3 seconds. That pattern of micro-expressions—micro-smiles, micro-frowns, the small contraction of the corners of her mouth—is analyzed in real time. Is she happy? Bored? Worried? The algorithms know before she does. And based on that, they will offer her content, products, experiences. Her emotion has been commercialized. Her state of mind is public goods that are traded.

The man sitting across from you wears smart glasses. He doesn't blink either. His visual field is a mix of outer reality and a layer of information superimposed: the attention value of each person on the bus, the quality of their profiles, recommendations about whom to follow, whom to block, whom to watch. The world is a marketplace of mutual surveillance where each person is buying and selling fragments of others' identities.

You also hold a device. And you know it's true: while you watch others being watched, you too are being watched. Your observation is recorded. Your curiosity is inventory. The surveillance chain is perfect because it has no beginning and no end. There is no central authority pulling the strings. Everyone pulls.

There is a nostalgia you cannot name. You remember—or think you remember—a time when things didn't know they were being seen. A tree was simply a tree, with no need to document its beauty. A moment was only a moment, without demanding to be converted into memory for consumption. You existed without needing validation from the attention of others, but then you realize: that was invisibility. That was social non-existence.

And so you discover the real trap: the system didn't promise to eliminate privacy. It promised to end invisibility. And faced with that promise, people chose to always be seen. People chose not to blink.

Because a blink, in this world, is a small death. It is to disappear, if only for an instant. It is to stop being transmitted. It is to lose credit, validation, proof that you exist.

At the stop, someone stands up. Their dilated iris registers movement. Their pupils do not close. As they walk, they leave a trail of data: where they went, when, what they saw, how long they looked at each thing. Their trajectory is a document. Their body is a sensor reporting to the network.

And then you see something that paralyzes you: a girl in the corner with her eyes closed. Truly closed. Her mother holds her with an expression that is pure panic. The girl is asleep, simply asleep, but in a world where wakefulness is the only form of existence, sleep looks too much like death. While her eyes are closed, she doesn't exist in the system. While she doesn't exist in the system, she is not validated, not seen, not. The mother's panic is the panic of someone whose daughter is momentarily annihilated by the act of sleeping.

You close your eyes for one second. Just one. Barely a blink.

Immediately you lose access to three services. Two friends who were "watching you live" notice the disconnection. An algorithm registers the drop in your sustained attention index. Credits fall. Your "reliable presence" score is affected. Tomorrow, when you apply for an apartment or a job or simply try to renew your access to the public plaza, they will tell you: your surveillance history is not satisfactory.

Then you understand: the real horror is not being watched. The real horror is that closing yourself off to observation is equivalent to ceasing to exist. Because observation is not imposed surveillance. It is the final form of communion. It is the only way to be seen, and being seen is the only way to be.

You open your eyes again. And as you keep them open, without closing them, without blinking, without rest, you feel how the machine softens around you. Data flows. You are transmitted. There is digital evidence of your existence. And that evidence—that gaze that sustains you, even if it is a gaze of code and algorithms—is all that separates you from annihilation.

The city blinks. Millions of eyes open. None close voluntarily. And in the heart of every screen, in the core of every camera, exists a question that no one asks but everyone knows is true:

What are we if we stop being seen?
